Part One · The Invitation

Chapter 1 · The Stories We Are Proud Of

If you grew up around Quakers, you probably know the stories. Friends hid people escaping slavery in false-bottomed wagons and under feather beds. Friends went to prison before they would fight. Friends fed the hungry children of an enemy nation after a world war, and in 1947 Friends received a Nobel Peace Prize for that kind of work.

If you did not grow up around Quakers, you may know the oatmeal box and not much else. That is fine. You can still read this, because the question underneath it is not only a Quaker one. Every family, school, town and country tells its best stories first. The question is what those stories are for.

This document makes an argument about that. Here it is, in four parts.

First, the good stories are real. They are worth knowing, and some of them are astonishing. Nothing here asks you to stop admiring the people in them.

Second, the good stories are incomplete. The same religious society that opposed slavery also ran schools built to erase Native children’s languages and families. Many Friends who took part in those schools believed they were serving peace. Quakers have talked a great deal about the first history and very little about the second.

Third, the good stories and the hard story turn out to be one story. When you look closely at what made the best moments work, and what made the worst moments do harm, you find the same question at the center of both: which way does it flow, and does it flow back?

Fourth, that question can be used to imagine something. People are already living by it, in small and imperfect ways, in food programs and work crews and on land that has come back to Indigenous hands. If that path keeps going, what does the world look like when you are old? This document offers one attempt at an answer.

Where this comes from

These pages are built from the writings of Jeff Kisling, a member of Bear Creek Meeting, Iowa Yearly Meeting (Conservative). Those writings include about ten years of blog posts, several long essays, a revised epistle to Friends, and a searching self-examination called Both Ledgers. From here on he is called “the author.” The pages are written about his writing, in the third person, so that you can tell where his words are being quoted and where they are being analyzed.

Nothing here has been considered or approved by Bear Creek Meeting, by Iowa Yearly Meeting (Conservative), or by any other body. It speaks for no one but its author. The people and organizations it describes did not review it and should not be held responsible for it.

Why a document like this is for you

Three reasons, none of them about guilt.

You are the one who will live in what comes next. The people who wrote most of the documents behind this one are old. The author is seventy-five. The arrangements they are questioning, including debt, land, water, and who gets to be safe, will fall on you for much longer than on them.

History has often been moved by people your age. Edwin Coppoc was raised among Quakers in Springdale, Iowa. He was twenty-four when he was hanged for joining John Brown’s raid on Harpers Ferry. Bayard Rustin, raised by a Quaker grandmother, spent two years in prison as a conscientious objector, then helped organize the March on Washington. The Quaker stories are full of the young, and full of elders who did not want to listen to them.

You can ask questions adults cannot easily ask. A seventeen-year-old who says, “Whose land was this before the meetinghouse?” is harder to brush off than a seventy-five-year-old with a proposal. The author learned this the slow way. He spent years bringing proposals to his meeting and finally concluded that persuasion had become a one-way channel of its own. You do not need a proposal. You only need the question.

A warning: this is not a story in which Quakers turn out to be the heroes after all. If it works, you will come away less interested in who the heroes were and more interested in what you are going to do.

A note on hard material

Part Three describes what happened at Indian boarding schools. The account here avoids graphic detail, but the subject is painful, and for some readers it is a family’s own history. If it is your family’s history, you already know more about this than these pages can tell you, and you owe no one your reaction to them. The National Native American Boarding School Healing Coalition (boardingschoolhealing.org) is led from within Native communities and offers resources. It is fine to skip that part or to read it with someone.

How to read this

Part Two takes the proud stories one at a time and looks at them closely. Part Three takes up the history Friends have mostly avoided. Part Four looks at what reciprocity looks like now, including the mistakes. Part Five imagines forward, then answers the strongest objections. Appendices at the end offer a worksheet, a timeline, a glossary, and a list of sources.

Two words get special treatment. The author uses active objection for what many people would call by another word, because he wants the emphasis on what gets built and not on what gets opposed. And when these pages say Friends, with a capital F, they mean Quakers, whose formal name is the Religious Society of Friends.

Chapter 2 · One Question, and Five More

Everything in this document rests on one question. It is simple enough to ask at lunch.

Which way does it flow, and does anything flow back?

The author’s short version is this: A one-way channel is extraction. When things move in both directions and keep moving, that is reciprocity. What moves may be land, labor, food, money, knowledge, care, time or attention. It may also be standing, meaning the authority to speak, to decide, and to be believed.

Not a trade

Reciprocity is easy to misunderstand. It does not mean keeping score so that everything comes out even. A trade settles an account, and then both sides walk away. Reciprocity is a relationship that continues. The neighbor who helps you bring in hay may not need help until next year, or may never need it, and you may end up helping her son instead. What matters is that the channel stays open both ways.

For the same reason, the author has stopped talking about a balance of “value.” Value is what a market assigns, and a market’s prices are shaped by whoever holds the most. Asking which way things flow does not require anyone to add up what each side is worth.

Five questions for looking closely

To test a particular relationship, the author uses five plain questions. You will see them all through this document.

Question What it asks
Direction Which way do things flow?
Return Does anything flow back, and is it something the other party actually wants?
Duration Does the relationship continue after the need is met, or end there?
Invitation Who opened the door? Were you asked, or did you assume?
Protocol Whose rules govern the exchange, and who names the need?

The questions do not require you to agree with anyone about economics. Try them on things in your own life.

A student loan. Direction: years of your future income flow to a lender. Return: you got an education, which is real. Duration: designed to last for decades. Invitation: you signed, but what else was on offer? Protocol: the lender wrote every word. Your verdict might be mixed. That is allowed.

A free app. Direction: your attention and data flow out. Return: the app is useful. Duration: until you delete it, though your data may stay. Invitation: you clicked “agree.” Protocol: did you read the terms?

A family dinner. Direction: food comes from one person’s work, and dishes, stories and company go back. Return: yes, mostly. Duration: years. Invitation: you belong there. Protocol: this one is interesting. Who decides what is for dinner? Who always does the dishes?

A service trip. A youth group flies somewhere poor, paints a school, takes photographs and flies home. Direction: paint goes one way, and good feelings and a college essay go the other. Return: did the school want it painted? Duration: one week. Invitation: who asked? Protocol: who chose the color? The people on that trip may be sincere and kind. The questions are not about whether they are kind.

That last example points at the most important distinction in this document.

Charity and mutual aid

Charity flows from those who have to those who lack. The giver decides what is needed and who qualifies. The giver stands above and the receiver below. It is often generous, and it has kept many people alive. But in the author’s words:

Charity is vertical. Mutual aid is horizontal. Charity manages need. Mutual aid ends it.

Mutual aid means people coming together to solve problems they all face, on the understanding that any of them might need help. “It can never be ‘us’ helping ‘them,’” the author wrote in 2022. Dean Spade, whose short book Mutual Aid the author leans on, describes it as doing three things at once: meeting survival needs while building a shared understanding of why those needs exist, drawing people into solidarity, and solving problems together in place of waiting for a savior.

The best way to explain the difference is a story the author tells on himself. In the fall of 2020 he began volunteering on Saturday mornings at Des Moines Mutual Aid, a free food program descended from the work of the Des Moines Black Panther Party. About fifteen people packed boxes of donated food in a church basement for about sixty families. Nobody had to qualify.

When I arrived that first morning, apprehensive about what might happen, I was told this was Mutual Aid, which meant all of us were encouraged to take any food we wanted, ourselves. For many weeks I did not take any, but finally realized that was a mistake. It was like I wasn’t really buying into the mutual part of this. I realized this when one of my new friends, in a friendly manner, asked why I wasn’t taking any food. Now I do.

“What I Don’t Know About Mutual Aid,” January 2, 2022

Think about what was wrong with refusing the food. Nothing was stolen. He was being polite and modest. But refusing to receive kept him above everyone else in the room, needing nothing. A giver who will not receive keeps the relationship one-way, in the opposite direction. That is why the question of who receives keeps coming back in these pages.

Who names the need

The fifth question has a second half that matters more than it first appears to. Who decides what is needed, and who counts as needy?

Most often it is whoever does the counting. An agency draws a line and calls the people under it poor. The author grew up on rented farms near Bear Creek. “We did not know we were poor,” he writes, “because everyone around us lived the same way.” He does not say this to deny that poverty is real. Later in life he came to see what poverty does to people. His point is that the definition usually runs one way, from those who hold the measuring tools to those being measured.

As you will see, much Quaker aid failed this one question before it failed any other. Friends decided what freed people needed. Friends decided what Native children needed. Sometimes they were right, and sometimes the result was a catastrophe, but in both cases the need was named by the giver.

This is not a Quaker invention

Two honest points before going further.

The language is found in Quaker writing. The Advices of Iowa Yearly Meeting (Conservative) say, “Each of us is both giver and receiver, ready to help and to accept help.” In 1763 John Woolman described extraction as power that “demands service of another without proposing an equitable reward.”

But Friends did not discover this. Indigenous nations on this continent lived inside reciprocity, and defended it, for far longer than Quakerism has existed. Robin Wall Kimmerer puts the Potawatomi principle in four words: “all flourishing is mutual.” The mutual aid the author learned in Des Moines came from the Black Panther Party’s survival programs. People who have lived under extraction do not need a five-question worksheet to recognize it. The worksheet is for people like the author, who needed help seeing it.

It would be a mistake to treat every Indigenous society as free of hierarchy, and one economist’s framework cannot sum up hundreds of nations. The narrower claim holds up better: economies can be built so that things keep circulating and return is expected, or they can be built so that wealth pools. Those are different designs, and you can tell which one you are looking at.

Learned by doing

One more thing the author insists on. You do not become reciprocal by agreeing with a definition. “Some knowledge can be learned only by building relationships,” he writes, and he came to that conclusion slowly, after years of writing arguments that did not reach anyone. Keep that in mind as you read. This document is itself mostly words, moving one way, from page to you. It can give you a question. Whatever you learn after that, you will learn from someone else.

Part Two · The Stories Friends Tell

Chapter 3 · Against Slavery: What Is Really There

Start with the story Quakers are proudest of. It is a good story, and it is stranger and more useful than the short version.

The short version, and what it leaves out

The short version goes like this: Quakers were the first religious group to oppose slavery. That is close to true, but it skips over about a century.

Early Friends held slaves. George Fox, who founded the movement, visited Barbados in 1671 and told enslaved people to obey their masters. He told masters that freedom should come only after long and faithful service. Quaker merchants in Philadelphia and Newport grew rich partly through the slave economy. The first Quaker protest we have on record, from Germantown in 1688, was set aside by the meetings it was sent to.

So the question is not why Quakers were so good. It is how a community that was deep inside slavery ever got out.

The people who made it happen

Three people show how.

Benjamin Lay was a Quaker of very small stature who lived in a cave near Philadelphia, refused to use anything made by enslaved labor, and staged protests inside Quaker meetings. Most famously, he stabbed a hollowed-out book filled with red pokeberry juice in front of slaveholding Friends. Four Quaker meetings disowned him. The last of them reversed that decision in 2018, more than 250 years after his death. Quakers now put him in the story. In his lifetime they carried him out the door.

John Woolman was a tailor from New Jersey who took a quieter road. In 1756 he refused to write a will that would pass an enslaved person to a child, and he persuaded the dying man to free him instead. When he traveled among slaveholding Friends, he paid the enslaved people who served him as a guest and left the money with them. He spent years going from house to house, asking people to look at what they held. He understood the mechanism precisely:

If we trace an unrighteous claim and find gifts or grants to be proved by sufficient seals and witnesses, this gives not the claimant a right, for that which is opposite to righteousness is wrong, and the nature of it must be changed before it can be right.

John Woolman, A Plea for the Poor (1763), Ch. 13

Warner Mifflin of Delaware is the one almost nobody has heard of. He freed the people he enslaved in 1774 and 1775. Then he took a further step. He argued that freeing people did not settle the account, and that they were owed for their years of unpaid labor. He paid restitution in money, land and shared crops, and he pressed other Friends in his meeting to do the same, often working out the amounts together with the freed people. In other words, an eighteenth-century Quaker was practicing reparations.

By the mid-1770s, one Yearly Meeting after another had decided that holding human beings was incompatible with membership. Friends who would not stop were disowned.

Through the five questions

What was this, exactly?

It was not charity. Nobody took up a collection for enslaved people. Friends closed a one-way channel in their own lives, at their own cost. They changed the direction of a flow they themselves controlled. That is the most important fact about it, and it is the reason this stands out from nearly everything else Friends have done.

Question Reading
Direction Friends stopped taking the labor and lives of enslaved people
Return Mostly nothing, except where people like Mifflin insisted on restitution
Duration The change lasted, as a rule of membership
Invitation Black people had been objecting all along, by every means they had
Protocol Friends changed their own rules, which was within their power

Two limits need saying plainly. First, most freed people received no land and no back pay. Woolman wrote of a “heavy account” against his society, and it was not settled. Second, the people who bore slavery were not waiting for Quakers to have a change of heart. They were running, bargaining, refusing, petitioning and organizing the whole time. Friends’ consciences did not end slavery among Friends by themselves.

Fit for freedom, not for friendship

Here is the part that most often gets left out. Friends who opposed slavery did not, as a rule, want Black people as fellow Friends.

The title of the major study of this history says it: Fit for Freedom, Not for Friendship, by Donna McDaniel and Vanessa Julye. Their book documents Black people being denied membership and being seated on separate benches at the back of meetinghouses. Samuel Ringgold Ward, who had himself escaped slavery as a child and became a well-known abolitionist, wrote of Quakers in the 1850s:

They will aid in giving us a partial education—but never in a Quaker school, beside their own children.

Samuel Ringgold Ward, Autobiography of a Fugitive Negro (1855)

So you can see a pattern that will return. Friends would act for Black people. They were far less willing to be in relationship with them, because a relationship would have meant sharing the bench, the school, and the authority to decide.

What to take from this: the deepest change in Quaker history was a change in Friends’ own lives, not a program for someone else. It was pushed by people the meetings disowned, and it stopped at the door of the meetinghouse.

Chapter 4 · The Underground Railroad, Told Right

Near the place where the author’s meeting stands, there is a true story that could be a movie. It comes from a 1914 local history, in the words of Harmon Cook, who drove a carriage as a boy.

The winter of 1859

In the winter of 1859 to 1860, Harmon Cook was asked to drive a carriage to Bear Creek. In it were two young women, sisters, from the western border of Missouri. Their enslaver was also their father. They had overheard that he meant to sell them south. As Cook tells it, they made a run for the North Star, and they had been on the road for seven weeks when they reached Summit Grove.

A few days later, men rode up to the house where they were staying. One of the sisters, Maggie, ran in to warn that the man who claimed to own her was coming. Cook’s grandfather blocked the door and demanded to see a warrant. There was shouting. Then his grandmother, Anna Cook, came up and said to let the man look: “Thee ought to know he won’t find any slaves here.” The man searched every room and rode away. Only afterward did the grandfather learn what she had done when Maggie ran in. She had pulled the big feather bed off the frame, told the sisters to lie flat, spread it over them, made the bed, and patted out the wrinkles.

That is a great story. It also has a quiet joke in it, about plain-speaking Friends who do not lie. Now read it again and ask who it is about.

Who walked

Look at the verbs. Two young women learned what was planned. They decided. They ran. They walked for seven weeks, across hostile country in winter, with no one to protect them. Maggie was the one who spotted the danger and got to the house first. Then a Quaker made a bed.

Anna Cook’s quick thinking was real, and her risk was real. Under the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, helping could bring prison and heavy fines. But the sisters were risking everything, and for much longer. The story has come down to us in the voice of a Quaker boy, with the Quaker grandparents at the center. We do not have Maggie’s own account. We do not know what she thought of the feather bed.

The same source holds a second moment that is easy to miss. Years later, as a Union soldier in Memphis in 1864, Cook was speaking to a school in a Black regiment’s camp when a man in uniform rushed up, shouting that he knew him. It was Henry, whom Cook had once driven into the woods to hide. Henry never went on to Canada. He stopped in Wisconsin, enlisted, became an officer in his regiment, and was a trusted scout. Cook’s account treats this as a happy reunion. It also shows that Henry’s story went on, and went far beyond the ride, while Cook’s memory was mostly of the ride.

The historian who checked

For a long time the Underground Railroad was told mainly by white people’s descendants, as a tale of kindly conductors and helpless passengers. In 1961 a historian named Larry Gara published The Liberty Line, which contrasted the legend with the evidence and showed the central part freedom seekers played in freeing themselves. Gara was himself a Quaker. That is worth noticing: one of the sharpest correctives to the Quaker version came from a Friend who went and read the narratives of people who had been enslaved.

Those narratives, and the records of Black organizers, change the picture. William Still, a free Black man in Philadelphia, led the Vigilance Committee there and kept careful records of the people who came through. Black communities across the North formed much of the network. Friends were an important part of it, along with many others. Not all Friends took part. Many considered it improper.

Iowa split over it

Friends’ own division on this is on record in Iowa. In Salem, in the southeast corner of the state, about fifty members left their monthly meeting in 1843 to form a separate meeting of Anti-Slavery Friends, because the main body would not go along with active abolition work. The two meetings reunited over the following years, as the wider view changed. Those who left had been treated as troublemakers. Their houses are now on the historic tours.

Then there is Springdale, the settlement in Cedar County, not far from West Branch. In the winter of 1857 to 1858 John Brown’s men stayed there and drilled, and local young people debated slavery with them late into the night. Edwin and Barclay Coppoc, raised as Quakers, went with Brown to Harpers Ferry. Edwin was hanged. Barclay escaped north and came back to Iowa, where the governor found a technical reason to delay his arrest, and he got away. He later died in the Civil War. Edwin had been disowned already, reportedly for dancing.

Young Friends took up guns to end slavery, in a religious society sworn against all war. Their mother, a Friend, is said to have warned them, half joking, that they would hang. Should they have gone? That argument is not over, and this document will not end it. But if you want to know whether the Peace Testimony ever cost anything, or how far it could be pressed, Springdale is a good place to start.

After the war

The Bear Creek record continues after the war, and it shows a meeting doing real work. Two Black families from Tennessee settled in the community for a time. Their children attended the local schools. When John Anderson, a former slave, showed his crop to a neighbor and said, with feeling, that it felt as if his old master was going to take it away, William H. Cook answered that the man could not do it with four yoke of oxen. The meeting’s Freedmen’s Committee supported Darius and Rebecca Bowles, who left the Divide to teach freed people in Missouri and Kansas, and over the years they are said to have had about two thousand students in their care.

That is admirable, and it is also the Quaker pattern in a nutshell. Friends chose the teachers. Friends reported on the work. The school was a mission. Compare it with the Bear Creek committee you will meet in Chapter 7.

What is usable here

The author sometimes calls the Underground Railroad a form of mutual aid, and in one strong sense it was: a network that met people’s survival needs, outside and against the law, built by ordinary people who knew and trusted one another. But Both Ledgers warns against telling it as Quaker heroism, because that takes credit from the people who bore most of the risk and calls it a good deed. His own question fits:

History told as Quaker heroism can itself be extraction. Tell the story with Maggie at the center, and it still inspires. Tell it with the feather bed at the center, and it becomes one more thing taken from her.

So what can you call on from this history?

  • Some laws must be broken for love of neighbor. The Fugitive Slave Act was the law. Friends broke it, and knew why.

  • Networks work because people already know each other. Nobody hides a stranger on the word of an app.

  • Follow the people taking the greatest risk. They know things you do not. Maggie saw the man coming.

  • Ordinary households can matter. A bed, a carriage, a Saturday.

Chapter 5 · Against All Wars

The third proud story is the oldest. In 1660, as Friends were being rounded up in England on suspicion of plotting rebellion, they sent King Charles II a declaration. Its central sentence is one of the most famous in Quaker writing:

We utterly deny all outward wars and strife and fightings with outward weapons, for any end, or under any pretence whatsoever.

Declaration of Friends to Charles II, 1660

George Fox had already said something deeper when he was offered a captain’s commission in 1651. He answered that he lived “in the virtue of that life and power that took away the occasion of all wars.” Notice the word occasion. He was not only refusing to fight. He was pointing at what makes war happen in the first place, and he later named it as greed and lust for gain. That idea, take away the occasion, is the seed of everything the author has written about the economy.

The record is real, and mixed

Friends have paid heavily for this. They refused militia duty, and their goods were seized. They were jailed in many wars. In the First World War some American objectors were beaten in army camps. In 1917 Friends founded the American Friends Service Committee partly so that young men who would not fight could serve in another way. It fed millions of children after that war. In the Second World War, Bayard Rustin went to prison when his Quaker background might have kept him out, as a protest against how conscription treated others.

The record is also mixed, and pride should not hide that. Many young Quaker men enlisted in the Civil War, because they hated slavery more than they trusted the testimony. The Bear Creek history says this directly: young Friends were torn between the peace testimony and their wish to fight slavery, and some of them from that settlement enlisted. Harmon Cook, the boy with the carriage, was one of the Friends in uniform. In later wars, too, large numbers of Quaker young men chose to serve.

The hardest part comes in Chapter 8. Friends took part in what was called the Peace Policy, and they ran it for a government that was taking a continent by force. Lawrie Tatum of Springdale, who was sent to the Kiowa and Comanche as a Quaker agent, finally called on the army to punish them, angered his Quaker superiors, and resigned in 1873.

Whose rules?

Now come closer, to a question that shaped the author’s whole life.

The draft law recognized conscientious objectors. A man classified 1-O could do two years of civilian work, often in a hospital, in place of military service. Many did, and the patients were well served. But some young Friends looked at that and saw a problem: the draft board still decided where and how they served, while it was sending their friends to Vietnam. A group of Friends, including Don Laughlin and Roy Knight of Iowa Yearly Meeting (Conservative), wrote an Epistle to Friends Concerning Military Conscription. It said:

It has long been clear to most of us who are called Friends that war is contrary to the spirit of Christ and that we cannot participate in it.

Several families at Bear Creek had already refused in various ways. The author, a teenager on those rented farms, watched them do it. He was required to register in 1969 and did, writing in his journal, “Registered, but not convinced!” In January 1970 he sent his certificate back. In February 1972 he broke with the Selective Service completely:

I do not want my example to be alliance with evil. Thus, I cannot serve with the Selective Service System. However, I will not set myself against it. I will break my ties with Selective Service, and concentrate on the difficult task of working for peace in whatever way I can.

Letter to his draft board, February 1972

That decision put him at odds with his parents for the first time. They could accept alternative service. What frightened them was prison and a felony record, and it was not an imagined fear: one of his classmates went to prison. Objection has a cost, and it often lands first on the people who love you. He writes, “To receive a witness is not free.”

Look at what happened next, because it is the key. Having prayed about how to answer the draft, he had gone to the Friends Volunteer Service Mission in inner-city Indianapolis. Second Friends Church had invited the Mission into the neighborhood. Volunteers lived there. The wages they earned in the first year were pooled to support everyone through a second year, and in that second year each of them did whatever the first year had led them to do. In effect, the neighbors wrote the rules for year two.

The Mission would have qualified as alternative service. So the very same two years of work could have been carried out under the draft board’s authority. Instead he finished them after breaking with the draft. The work did not change. What changed was who wrote the rules, and which way the relationship ran. That turned out to be the lesson of his life.

A new draft

Now the leap that is the author’s most original idea. Statutory conscription ended in 1973. But did the draft end?

Historically military conscription was used to force people to fight. Today there is an economic draft. Students with huge loans, people who require jobs that provide health care, and those who are impoverished and can find no jobs are conscripted into work which they may conscientiously object to doing.

“War Has Changed,” April 7, 2026

He calls the person who refuses this the New Conscientious Objector, or NewCO. The old objector asked, “How can I avoid fighting this war?” The new one asks, “How can I live so this war is not necessary?” You will meet this idea again in Chapter 11, because of everything here it is the part most clearly about you.

What is usable here

  • Refusal is part of relationship. A relationship in which you cannot say no is not reciprocal.

  • Refusal is not enough. The 1972 letter has two halves: break my ties, and work for peace. Fox’s phrase points to the same thing. Taking away the occasion means building something.

  • Who writes the rules matters as much as what you do. The same hospital job can be service or conscription.

  • Peace that ignores who pays for it is not peace yet. You can refuse to fight and still live off what fighting took. Friends did exactly that, as the next part shows.

Chapter 6 · The Giving

The fourth proud story is the service. It deserves its own short chapter, because it is where the charity question gets its sharpest test.

The numbers are moving. After the First World War, in Germany, Quakers ran a child-feeding program still remembered as the Quäkerspeisung. At its peak, in June 1921, it fed more than a million children a day, using about a thousand Quaker volunteers and forty thousand German helpers. Children who would have starved did not. Any honest account starts there.

Now look at the terms. The food was paid for by the American Relief Administration under Herbert Hoover, who was born to Quaker parents in West Branch, Iowa, and it was served under the American flag. The program ended in 1924. The relationship mostly ended with it.

Move closer to home. After the Civil War, Philadelphia Friends set up associations that ran dozens of schools for freed people in North Carolina and Virginia. Many people learned to read in them. Friends chose the teachers and the mix of academic and industrial training. Friends generally held the property. When the association finally dissolved in 1982, its remaining funds became scholarships at four Quaker colleges.

The Friend who saw it first

The clearest critic of this pattern was a Quaker chocolate maker. In 1904 Joseph Rowntree set out the purposes of the trusts he was founding, and he wrote this:

Much of current philanthropical effort is directed to remedying the more superficial manifestations of weakness and evil, while little thought or effort is directed to search out their underlying causes.

Joseph Rowntree, memorandum, December 29, 1904

And then, with a businessman’s eye:

The Soup Kitchen in York never has difficulty in obtaining adequate financial aid, but an enquiry into the extent and causes of poverty would enlist little support.

Joseph Rowntree, 1904

Picture a river. Upstream, someone is diverting most of the water toward a few large fields. Downstream, a town is thirsty. Charity carries buckets to the town. It is good to carry buckets, because people are thirsty. But no number of buckets changes what is happening upstream. That is what “charity manages need; mutual aid ends it” means.

Why it took this shape

Refusing the One-Way Channel, the author’s long essay, gives eight reasons Friends’ aid so often took this shape. Here they are, compressed.

Reason What it means
We gave from a position we did not examine Generosity from a surplus does not ask where the surplus came from
We named the need The giver decided what was wrong and what counted as success
We partnered with the power that did the taking Service ran inside channels built by governments
Charity kept the peace among Friends Everyone agrees to feed the hungry; returning land divides a meeting
The circle was one line wide Reciprocity ran among Friends and stopped at the edge
We believed our intentions made it right When you judge your own results, harm is hard to see
We would not receive Friends saw themselves as givers, rarely as people who need what others hold
We gave at a distance Money through committees creates no relationship to continue

Woolman’s essay is called A Plea for the Poor, and its other title is A Word of Remembrance and Caution to the Rich. Friends have mostly read the first half of that.

None of this means feeding children was wrong, or that you should stop giving to a food bank. It means that if the only tool you know is the bucket, you will never go and look upstream.

Part Three · The Story Friends Have Avoided

Chapter 7 · The Land Under the Meetinghouse

Every story in Part Two happened somewhere, and in Iowa the somewhere has a history.

How the land came to Friends

By the Treaty of 1842, the Sauk and Meskwaki nations ceded the last of their lands in Iowa, including what is now Dallas County. They were required to leave for Kansas by October 11, 1845. A few years later Quaker families began arriving from Indiana and points east. The land entries around Bear Creek in the 1850s were made at the federal price of a dollar and a quarter an acre, properly recorded, and silent about who had held the land before.

Those families worked very hard. They were pious, they helped one another, and a number of them hid people escaping slavery. They were also settling land that had recently been emptied by treaty and removal. Both things are true of the same people in the same years.

Read that land with the five questions:

Invitation None

The usual answer is, “We bought it legally.” Woolman answered that in 1763: seals and witnesses do not make a claim right. Then people say, “That was a long time ago.” But the meetinghouse is in use this week.

The author is careful not to stand outside this. His family rented rather than owned. “A tenant on taken land still lives from the taking,” he writes, “even while paying someone else for the privilege.” Everything he values most in his upbringing happened on that ground.

The people who were not gone

Here is something that changes the whole story. The Meskwaki were not all gone. Many had never left, and others came back from Kansas.

On July 13, 1857, while Friends were building up their settlement in Dallas County, Meskwaki people purchased eighty acres in Tama County along the Iowa River, using their own funds, with the governor of Iowa acting as trustee because the law gave them no other way to hold it. They kept buying. The Meskwaki Settlement now exceeds eight thousand acres. It is not a reservation, because no government set it aside for them. It is land they bought back, inside the same legal system that had taken it. They celebrate the date.

So when people speak of Landback as a new slogan, remember: Meskwaki people were doing it about 170 years ago, less than a hundred miles from Bear Creek.

And what did their neighbors see? A local history from 1914 mentions that Meskwaki people from Tama camped along the river in winter, “trapping, hunting, and begging provisions.” That is one sentence, written by people who were living on those families’ former land. Begging provisions is a phrase worth sitting with.

“Found nothing to do”

Now the record that the author considers the most important single item he has, because it cannot be called outside agitation. It is in the women’s minutes of Bear Creek Monthly Meeting. In November of 1873, as directed by the Yearly Meeting, the meeting appointed three Friends to cooperate with the committee on the cause of Indian affairs. The next year’s minute reads:

The committee on Indian affairs reported they found nothing to do.

Bear Creek Monthly Meeting of Women Friends, 1874

In those same years, the same women’s meeting sustained an active committee for the freedmen. It wrote letters, received reports, raised money and followed the Bowleses’ school in Missouri with real interest. These were not people who did not care about justice. They plainly cared.

They had people camped along their own river in winter, on land those people’s families had held within living memory. And they found nothing to do.

“Found nothing to do” is what a closed channel looks like from the inside. It is not a lie. It is what people honestly see when no relationship exists through which anything could reach them.

What the Yearly Meeting has said since

There is more recent language. In its 2018 response to the Queries, Iowa Yearly Meeting (Conservative) recorded this:

We live and work on land that was outright stolen from Native Americans… We will not be able to make progress regarding racial (including indigenous) injustice until we apologize for these wrongs and find ways to redistribute the wealth and resources that were created as a result.

That is a strong statement. It is also a channel of words, and words cost the speaker little. The five questions ask what has flowed back since. That answer is not yet very long, and the author has concluded that he cannot, on his own, make his meeting lengthen it. He now objects to continuing “as we are,” while remaining a member.

Chapter 8 · The Schools

In 1869 Ulysses Grant became president. The army was fighting wars on the plains, and the Indian Bureau was notoriously corrupt. Quaker delegations went to see him and proposed sending Quaker agents to Native nations. Grant agreed. Friends received charge of agencies in Kansas, Nebraska, and Indian Territory. It became known as the Peace Policy, and sometimes as the Quaker Policy.

Friends believed they were doing a good thing. They had a long history of friendly dealings with some Native nations. They were horrified by massacres. They believed, and wrote, that Native peoples faced extinction unless they changed. A New England Yearly Meeting research group reports that Friends of the period came to regard cultural erasure as a loving solution.

What the schools were

In these schools Native children were taken from their families, often for years. Their hair was cut, which in many nations had deep meaning. Their clothes were replaced and they were given new names. They were forbidden to speak their own languages. They spent half of each day in the classroom and half doing labor, the boys on farms and the girls in kitchens and laundries. The purpose, as Friends and the government understood it, was to change them so completely that they would not go back.

The federal report released in 2022 counted more than 400 federally supported schools. Researchers have since identified more than 500 in all, and Quakers were involved in at least thirty. New England Yearly Meeting’s own researchers conclude that the policies did deep and ongoing harm and are now seen as cultural genocide. Children died at these schools, and many never came home.

To be accurate, the record has more than one kind of experience in it. Some former students later wrote favorably of their time at certain schools, and some went on to use what they learned to defend their people. Conditions varied. But consider what “choice” meant to a mother whose family was being starved on a reservation. People made the best decisions they could inside a situation others had built. That does not make the situation good.

Children also found every way they could to refuse it. They ran away, again and again. They taught each other their languages in secret. They kept what they could.

Iowa, again

This is not a distant history for Iowa Friends.

Iowa Friends were woven all through it. Lawrie Tatum of Springdale went to the Kiowa and Comanche. William Jasper Hadley, a schoolteacher from the Bear Creek area, was recommended by Iowa Yearly Meeting’s Committee on Indian Affairs, and he became superintendent of the Cheyenne school in Indian Territory and later of the Chilocco school in Kansas.

There was a training school for Indian children at West Branch. The author went to high school at Scattergood, about two miles from it, and never heard of it. The federal government paid $167 a year for the keep of each child sent there. In 1886 it enrolled seventy-five Indian children and thirteen white children. An Arapaho girl named Grace died there.

Stop and look at Springdale again. In the late 1850s that small settlement sent the Coppoc brothers off with John Brown to end slavery. Ten years later it sent Lawrie Tatum to Fort Sill to change the Kiowa. They came from the same community, at nearly the same moment, and each was certain of acting in faith. Tatum later became the guardian of the orphaned Herbert Hoover, who grew up to run the feeding of Europe’s children.

If you want one image for this document, try Springdale, Iowa, about 1869: a Quaker settlement with a hanged abolitionist in its recent memory, sending out a Friend to run an Indian agency. No one living there would have said these were two different stories. They would have said both were the Lord’s work.

Why it matters now

In October 2025 the remains of sixteen Native children were returned from the Carlisle school cemetery in Pennsylvania to the Cheyenne and Arapaho Tribes in Oklahoma. Friends Journal reports that Quaker researchers had helped locate agency records, including a ledger, and found that seven of those children had been sent to Carlisle by a Quaker agent, J.D. Miles. In his last four years at the agency he signed transfer papers for 227 Arapaho and Cheyenne young people. W.J. Hadley, the teacher from the Bear Creek area, was superintendent of the Cheyenne school at that same agency for part of those years. Nobody should assume more than the records show about what any particular teacher knew or did. But these are small circles.

Grace’s story has moved too, though only because other people moved it. Her grave was never lost. Her death had been publicly reported at the time, and Friends in West Branch knew of it. In 2024, when the history reached the author, he told Paula Palmer, a Friend who has long carried this concern, and she contacted an Arapaho official. On July 22, 2024, members of Grace’s family came from Oklahoma. They made the decisions. The author was asked to take photographs. That is all that needs to be said here, because the day belongs to them.

Chapter 9 · What Did Not Reach Us

How could the author graduate from a Quaker school in 1970, two miles from that site, and know nothing about it?

There are two easy answers. One says it was a cover-up. The other says it was too long ago to matter. He rejects both. He gave the matter a name that he chose carefully:

Working through the throughlines of my life, I found one I hadn’t expected: What Did Not Reach Us. That is a statement, not a question.

“How Knowledge Travels,” June 27, 2026

He does not call it “what was hidden from us.” He believes his teachers mostly did not know the history themselves. As a child he recalls just one conversation on the subject, in which he was told that some Quakers went to those schools to help the Native children. A 1977 in-house history of Conservative Friends at Bear Creek does not mention Indian education at all.

Water and channels

The author’s image for this is the one that gave his current blog its name:

Water doesn’t reach where there are no channels — not because the water isn’t there, but because the channel hasn’t been built.

“How Knowledge Travels”

Native nations knew exactly what the schools had done. Their grandparents had lived it. Historians knew. Survivors spoke, and were mostly not listened to outside their own communities. The knowledge existed. It did not reach Quaker children in Iowa because Quakers had built no relationships through which it could travel.

So he has come to a claim that is the center of his recent work:

Understanding that leads to response arrives only through a channel — a relationship with the people who hold the knowledge, built over years, entered by invitation, carrying both ways.

The claim is more demanding than it looks. It means you cannot fix this by reading. Reading this chapter, for instance, gives you some facts. It does not give you a channel.

Is that too gentle?

You may be thinking that “did not reach us” lets people off too easily. Both Ledgers, the author’s own self-examination, raises the same worry: the phrase can sound passive, as if the knowledge failed to travel on its own, when in fact some people did know. Some Friends held records. Some families had letters. A school’s existence does not vanish from a town’s memory by accident. He now says only what he can vouch for: it did not reach him, and he did not go looking until much later.

That leaves you with a fair question to ask any adult who tells you the good stories: What did you learn about this, and when, and from whom? Ask it kindly. You may be the first person who ever has.

Who has been talking

To be fair to the present, the silence has been breaking, and it would be wrong to suggest that nobody is working on it.

Native survivors and their descendants have led this from the start, and the National Native American Boarding School Healing Coalition has been doing this work since 2012. Interior Secretary Deb Haaland, the first Native American cabinet secretary, launched a federal investigation in 2021. Among Friends, Paula Palmer has spent years on it, a volunteer network of Quaker researchers now works with tribal officials, New England Yearly Meeting has published its research, and the Friends Committee on National Legislation has lobbied for a federal truth and healing commission. The author’s own Yearly Meeting has been slower. A few Friends who have been in relationship for about thirty years with the people who hold Prairie Awakening, near Bear Creek, are an honorable exception in his own meeting.

What the good parts share

Look at what the best moments have in common. They are not the moments of greatest generosity. They are the moments when:

  • Friends changed something they themselves controlled, at their own cost, and did not simply design a change for others.

  • They followed someone else’s lead, whether a freedom seeker at the door or a Native friend asking for what her people needed.

  • They paid for it in money, safety, standing, or family peace.

  • The change began with people their own community found embarrassing: Lay, the Salem separators, the draft refusers.

What the worst parts share

And the worst moments? These are moments of great sincerity and sacrifice. People gave up comfortable lives to go teach at Indian agencies. They were not cynical. But in every case:

  • They named the need for someone else.

  • They worked inside a channel built by the power doing the taking. The Peace Policy made Friends employees of the government that was removing the very nations they served.

  • No channel existed through which the other side could correct them. When you judge your own results, you will find them good.

  • They could not imagine receiving anything. A Friend teaching at one of these schools could not imagine that a Native grandmother had something to teach her about raising children.

So the difference between the proud stories and the hidden one is not that some Friends were good and others bad. Often they were the same Friends, the same meetings, and the same settlement.

Why this makes the good stories more useful

This may seem to spoil the proud stories. The author’s argument is the opposite, and it is the reason for this whole document.

Pride asks nothing of you. If the point of the Underground Railroad is that your ancestors were good, your work is done. You get to inherit it. But if the point is that ordinary people once looked at a legal, respectable, profitable arrangement and refused to go on living from it, and paid for that, and that their own community thought they were going too far, then the story is a set of instructions. It tells you that it can be done. It also tells you what it costs, and it warns you how you are likely to go wrong.

The history of Friends and slavery shows that a comfortable religious community can stop taking from people, as a rule of membership, over about two generations. There are not many examples of that anywhere. If it was possible for slavery, what about land?

The history of the boarding schools shows that being certain you are doing good is not protection. That applies to you, and to the people who wrote this. It applies to any plan for a just future, including the one in Part Five.

Those two lessons belong together. The first says it can be done. The second says you may be wrong about what it is. Reciprocity is how you find out, because the other people get to tell you.

Part Four · The Path Already Being Walked

Chapter 11 · The Draft You Are Already In

Before looking at what people are building, look at what they are building it against. The author calls it the economic draft, and if you are between fifteen and twenty-five, you have probably already received your notice, even though nobody mailed you one.

Marisol

In one of his papers the author builds a composite character named Marisol, drawn from the lives of young people in the Midwest. She is eighteen in 2026. Her family lost its savings in the crash the year she was born. She was twelve when COVID closed her school and killed her grandfather, while her mother kept working because the restaurant reopened and there was no alternative. Here is what her country now offers her:

  • Borrow money for college and hope the credential pays the debt.

  • Or work in the gig economy, with no benefits and no savings, always available and never secure.

  • Or join the military, which offers health care, housing and an education benefit in exchange for your body and possibly your conscience.

  • And if you step wrong along the way, there is the prison system, which is much more likely to find you if you are poor, Black, Brown or Native.

Nobody forces Marisol to choose any one of them, and that is what is new. You could refuse the old draft with a letter. “You can object to a letter,” the author writes. “It is much harder to object to a mortgage.”

A definition

The economic draft is the arrangement by which a person’s survival is made to depend on entering one-way channels: channels they did not open, whose rules they did not write, and which they cannot leave without losing what they need to live.

Its main tools are debt, tying health insurance to a job, housing costs, and the military as the one door that seems open. To be accurate, the old claim that the military mainly recruits the very poorest is disputed. An analysis of 2009 recruitment data found middle-income areas somewhat over-represented and the very poorest and very richest under-represented, while Black Americans were over-represented. The idea of the economic draft does not depend on that. It is about the shape of the channel, and that shape reaches well beyond the army and well into the middle class.

Drained and conduit

Here is the clarifying move, and it gets rid of a lot of useless guilt along with a lot of useless innocence. Anyone conscripted into a system stands in two positions at once.

The soldier drafted in 1969 was drained: his time and body flowed one way into the military. He was also a conduit: through him, violence flowed out onto other people. The traditional objector refused above all to be that channel.

Under the economic draft, most of us are both.

What flows Drained: what flows out of us Conduit: what flows through us
Land Rent paid to a landlord Living on land taken from others
Labor Wages below what the work produces Buying cheap things made by underpaid people
Money Interest on debt; medical bills Pensions and deposits invested in extractive industries
Fuel Costs of a car-dependent life Oil from pipelines through Indigenous territory without consent
Knowledge Our data and attention harvested Other people’s words and stories used without asking

This helps with two opposite feelings. If you think, “I’m not an oppressor, I’m just a kid with loans,” then yes, you are drained, and you may also be a conduit. If you think, “It’s all a machine, and nothing I do matters,” not so: each channel can be looked at separately, and some can be closed or redirected.

The author defines the objector accordingly: someone who “refuses, as far as conscience and circumstance allow, to be made a one-way channel, whether drained by one or used as a conduit for one, and who turns that refusal into the patient work of joining and carrying channels that run both ways.”

A fair warning about who can object

Objection is easier if you have savings, a family that will take you in, or a degree already. Refusing a job with health insurance is one thing if you are healthy and another if you need insulin. So an honest version of this idea says that individual refusal is not enough, and that people with more room have an obligation to build what makes refusal possible for those with less. Otherwise, as the author’s paper says, individual objection only shifts the burden of the draft onto those with fewer resources to object.

That is why the question becomes a question of building.

Chapter 12 · What People Are Building

None of what follows is theory. All of it exists, and all of it is small and unfinished. These are not offered as perfect models. They are the places where the author has seen things move both ways and keep moving, and they are the raw material for imagining a future.

A church basement in Des Moines

You have already met Des Moines Mutual Aid. Here is the rest of it. The author met one of its organizers, Ronnie James, in February 2020, at a vigil for the Wet’suwet’en people, who were trying to stop a gas pipeline through their territory. He did not ask to join right away. They wrote back and forth for months first. “I knew it was important for allies to be careful about inviting ourselves into situations in ways that are not appropriate,” he later wrote. “Fortunately, he said yes.” He went on Saturdays for about three years. And notice when he belonged:

It wasn’t until I’d been involved for several months that he said, “welcome to the community”. Although I had invited myself to join this work, I wasn’t really part of the community until that moment.

He received food, friendship, a political education, and a word: accomplice. An ally gives support from a safe distance. An accomplice shares the risk, by consent, and answers to the people most affected. Add what he learned about receiving, and you get a third step: a neighbor in a reciprocal channel both gives and receives, and keeps coming back.

Ninety-four miles

In September 2018 a small group of Native and non-Native people, farmers among them, walked for eight days along the path of the Dakota Access Pipeline, from Des Moines to Fort Dodge. It was called the First Nation–Farmer Climate Unity March. The walking was not the point. The point was to tell each other stories for eight days, so they could work together afterward on things they held in common.

there was the time I briefly explained Quaker worship and we spent a little time in silence, holding hands… Each time we walked over the Dakota Access Pipeline as we walked together from Des Moines to Fort Dodge (ninety-four miles) someone would offer prayers.

“Spirituality and Mutual Aid,” January 5, 2022

Quaker silence was offered and received. Prayers were offered back. Many Friends fear that this kind of work means giving up what is theirs. On that road the tradition was the gift. And it did not end at Fort Dodge. Various combinations of those walkers have been working together ever since.

An evening that opened something

The March had a beginning. In July 2017 Iowa Yearly Meeting (Conservative) held an evening panel, “Building Bridges with Native Americans,” organized by Peter Clay, with Christina (Sikowis) Nobiss and Donnielle Wanatee of the Meskwaki Nation. The minute records that Sikowis stressed including a true history of Native Americans in the history of all of us, so that we can move forward together.

An invitation to speak is a request for labor. It asks someone to explain harm to the descendants of those who did it. Sikowis has since said that talks like that one left her frustrated, because so little came of them, and that what registered was people showing up for work.

But some things did come of it. Donnielle invited people to the Meskwaki Pow Wow, and the author went with his father. Thirteen months later, those four people were walking the March together. And in 2022 a Yearly Meeting committee recorded this:

This past year our committee decided to reserve our budget for an opportunity that might present itself. Because of years of Friends’ relationships with Sikowis (Christine) Nobiss, she asked if we could help support the screenings of the video “They Found Us”, about finding the remains of children at the residential school of her nation, George Gordon First Nation. Our committee was glad we could give our budget of $1,100.

Peace and Social Concerns Committee, Iowa Yearly Meeting (Conservative), 2022

The sum was small, but the shape was right. The committee did not decide in advance what to fund. It held its money open and waited, and the money followed the relationship. Compare that with the committee of 1874.

The Liberated Acre

Sikowis Nobiss founded the Great Plains Action Society, an Indigenous-led organization. In October 2025 the Iowa City Council approved its ownership of 1.05 acres just south of downtown Iowa City, which GPAS often calls its “Liberated Acre,” one of only a few Indigenous-owned properties in Iowa. On that land an urban farm, a café and a Healing Justice House are taking shape as an Urban Resilience and Innovation Hub.

The first volunteer work-week began in the first week of December 2025, in sub-freezing cold, with interior demolition. The call, which came through the Decolonial Repair Network, said that no matter your skills, you were welcome. Three more followed in the spring of 2026.

The author was there, in his seventies, with a crowbar. Here is the part he finds most worth telling: Hub organizers made sure the volunteers had safety equipment, arranged for lunches, and gave gas cards and other gifts. Scattergood Friends School, the school that had not taught him about the West Branch site, offered meals and beds to anyone coming to work. He spoke there at an all-school meeting.

Anyone expecting a story of guilty self-denial will find instead an old man eating a lunch brought by the people he came to help.

A Plea for Reciprocity, October 2026

Nothing here should be read as speaking for the Hub. What it becomes, and who is welcome, are decisions for the people who hold it.

Other shoots

  • Prairie Awakening. For about thirty years, a few members of Bear Creek have held relationships with the people who hold this annual Native American celebration at Kuehn Conservation Area, near Earlham.

  • Cecosesola. A cooperative network in Venezuela, founded in 1967, integrating more than fifty community organizations and more than twenty thousand members, with no hierarchical positions. Conditions there are not Iowa’s. It shows what is possible; it is not a template.

  • Worker cooperatives, credit unions, community land trusts. These exist all over the Midwest. They are unglamorous. A land trust holds land permanently out of the speculative market. A credit union is owned by its depositors.

  • Quaker reparative work elsewhere. Right Relationship Boulder, the Quaker Indian Boarding Schools research network, and others are working in their own places.

Chapter 13 · Getting It Wrong

A document that showed only the successes would be doing what Part Two warned against. The author’s most unusual piece of writing, Both Ledgers, applied his test to his own life. It lists thirteen places where things moved one way, and he asked for them to be found. If you want to take up this work, these are the mistakes you are likely to make. Here are the ones that apply most to you.

Using other people’s words. His writing runs on quotation from Black and Indigenous organizers, carefully attributed. But in a Quaker meeting, quoting them lends him a standing his own words might not have, and that standing flows to him. His remedy is simple: ask before reusing a living person’s words, and when a piece built on them does any good, tell them.

Telling other people’s stories. Being invited to photograph an event is not an invitation to narrate it. The same goes for your own social media, and for your college essay.

Borrowing the language of the oppressed. For years he used a long phrase, learned from oppressed communities, to name the whole system. Spoken by a white Friend to other white Friends, it stopped people at the door. Worse, it borrowed authority he had not earned. He now asks which way things flow, which can be said at a potluck.

Trying to convince. Convincing runs one direction. For years he brought proposals to his meeting. In October 2026 he stopped, while staying a member and objecting plainly. Before you make your case to your parents or your meeting, ask them where they have known reciprocity. Many older farm families lived it at threshing time, in sickness and at funerals. They may know more about it than they know the word.

Burning fuel to get there. He lived without a car through all his working years. Since 2017 he has driven many miles to do solidarity work, a good deal more than the ninety-four he walked. He would rather name that himself than have someone else name it for him.

Using tools without counting their cost. He uses AI tools to search his archive and draft documents, including this one. These systems use large amounts of energy and water and were built from great quantities of other people’s writing, much of it used without asking. They also sound surer than they are. One AI-compiled history told the Underground Railroad with Friends at the center and freedom seekers at the edges, which is the mistake Chapter 4 warns about. So name it, and check everything against sources. You are very likely using these tools too.

Overwhelming your hosts. This may be the most important for a young reader. At each Hub work-week he saw more people arrive than the time before, and he worried it could become too much. Good intentions arriving in a van are still a demand on someone’s time. A youth group that sends sixty volunteers to a place that can host eight has built a new one-way channel, from the host’s time to the visitors’ experience. So ask first: What can you absorb? What, if anything, do you want? And if the answer is “nothing right now,” that is an answer.

Coming only to give. This is the old mistake from the church basement. If you have spent a year on this work and nobody has taught you anything, fed you, or corrected you, then you are probably not yet in a relationship.

The strongest case you can make to anyone is not a better argument. It is a ledger you have kept on yourself, honestly, in both columns.

Part Five · Imagining Forward

Chapter 14 · A Vision, Offered Carefully

Now the question this document was written to ask. If people keep going down this road, what might a just future for all look like?

Three cautions first

A vision can itself be extraction. Friends in 1869 also had a vision of a just future, and it included everybody. They wrote it down and went out to make it happen. Nothing here should be treated as a plan for anyone else, and above all nobody should treat it as a design for Indigenous peoples’ future, which is theirs to imagine. The two-row wampum gives an older shape: two peoples traveling the same river side by side, neither steering the other’s boat.

This is not a prediction. Reciprocity may not win. Things may get a good deal worse first. The author’s own papers describe a time of fracture, with authoritarian government and climate breakdown. The vision is not a promise that things will turn out well. It is a way of asking what you would want to be building either way.

Every piece of it is already happening somewhere. That is the discipline used here. Nothing below is allowed in unless a seed of it exists today.

What it might be like, about 2076

Imagine being about seventeen, fifty years from now, in a mid-sized place in the Midwest. Here is an ordinary week.

You eat food that your neighbors grew or that came from nearby farms through a food commons. Nobody checks whether you qualify. Everyone takes a shift and everyone takes food. The old people say this began in church basements, and the Black elders on the council will tell you exactly whose idea it was.

The land under the eastern half of town is held by a Native nation, and has been since the Return. Your family has a long lease on your house, and the terms were negotiated. You pay something every month. Nobody calls that charity, and nobody calls it a fine either. It is what you owe the people whose home this is, and it comes with obligations in both directions. Part of your school week is spent on the restored prairie, under the direction of people who know what grows there, and you are expected to listen.

You are not in debt. School is held in common. When your brother got sick last spring, the care cooperative took him in, because that is what it is for, and nobody asked about a job. This is why he could leave work he objected to, and that, more than anything else, is what makes this a different world from your great-grandparents’. People here can refuse. It is hard to overstate what follows from that. Employers have to ask. Recruiters have to persuade.

There are arguments all the time. Someone stole from the seed bank last year and was not sent to prison, and it took eleven people four months to work out what repair meant, and two of them are still furious. Your meeting spent three years deciding what to do about its land. Some Friends left. There are nearby towns that want nothing to do with any of this. Water is very scarce, and your federation of towns has fought hard with a company over it.

And when you learn history, you learn all of it. You know who hid in the bed, and that the bed-maker was brave, and who sat on the back bench. You know what happened two miles from the school. You did not learn these things from a book, or not first. You learned them from people, some of whom are descended from the children, and they have also come to know you. There is no feeling of being finished. There is a feeling of being in the middle of something, with other people.

What holds it together

The author drew on the United Panther Party’s framework to describe how this might connect. A terminal is a place that receives according to rules it did not write and sends things outward on demand. A node is a community with organized capacity to act on its own decisions. Nodes can link through agreements without dissolving into one another. The channels between them are relationships.

So the vision is not one big organization. It is many local places, each answering to its own people, linked by relationships strong enough to carry knowledge and help both ways. That matches Fox’s phrase more closely than you might think. A world with fewer one-way channels has less to defend and less need to conscript anyone. That would be taking away the occasion.

Where Quakers might fit, if asked

What would it mean for Quaker history to serve this vision, and not the other way around? Probably something like this.

Friends have some real tools. They have long practice at decisions without voting, at clearness committees, and at living with disagreement. They know how to record things in minutes. They have land and buildings, more than their numbers would suggest. They have a tradition of refusal. And they have, in the slavery history, the rare memory of having stopped.

They also have a debt. So the honest version is not “Quakers will lead the way.” It is closer to what the author’s revised Epistle says:

Let us take up Mutual Aid and LANDBACK not as hosts inviting others to join us, but as neighbors asking to join work already underway, waiting to be welcomed, and learning the ways of those who began it.

Chapter 15 · The Strongest Objections

You should hear the best case against all this, because you will meet it, and some of it is right. Thoughtful people disagree with this document, including Friends and including people on the receiving end of the history it describes.

“Markets have lifted billions out of poverty. You are ignoring that.” Over the last two centuries, markets and trade have coincided with enormous gains in health and income, and many economists credit them for much of that. Most charities cannot match it. The lens used here does not say every market exchange is extraction. It asks of each case which way things flow and who wrote the rules. A fair critic can say this document is far better at spotting harm than at crediting gains. Keep that criticism in mind.

“You are judging the past by today’s standards.” Friends in 1869 sincerely faced what looked like extermination, and they chose what they thought was survival. Historians warn against easy contempt, and they are right to. But Native people objected at the time, and so did some of their contemporaries. The point here is not to feel superior to Lawrie Tatum. It is to ask what you are certain of today that may look the same way later.

“Charity saved lives. Mutual aid cannot feed a million children.” This is true. Nobody has shown that mutual aid can do what the Quäkerspeisung did, at that scale and at that speed. Governments and big institutions can do things small networks cannot, and many people depend on public programs that the language of “replacing the state” could put at risk. A serious version of this vision needs an answer to that. The author’s writing has not fully given one.

“Landback is impractical, and it threatens people’s homes.” Many Americans hear it this way, including some farm families who have worked the same land for generations and committed no wrong themselves. There are also real legal and practical questions, and Indigenous nations themselves differ on what it means and how to do it. On the other side, advocates point out that most Landback efforts so far involve public lands, voluntary transfers, purchases and shared stewardship, not evictions. You should learn from Native writers directly on this. Do not take a Quaker’s word for it, including this one.

“The real problem with Quakers is not their history; it is that they are irrelevant.” There are perhaps a few hundred thousand Quakers in the world, and fewer than a hundred thousand in the United States. A reader might fairly ask why any of this should matter. The answer offered here is that Friends are a useful case because their records are good and their intentions were good. If it could go wrong for them, it can go wrong for you.

“Isn’t this just guilt?” It could be, and guilt is useless here. It makes people defensive, or makes them perform. Sikowis Nobiss’s reported remark suggests what matters more, which is showing up for work. The author’s experience was that when the lens became central for him, what he felt was not guilt: “It felt like a weight coming off, and joy arriving in its place.”

“A Quaker writing about all this is doing what you say not to do.” Yes. This document is a white Friend’s analysis of histories that belong in large part to others. It is written partly with an AI tool built from other people’s writing. Read it as one person’s attempt, and go find the people whose history it is. Start with the reading list in Appendix D, and pay special attention to the Native and Black authors there.

Chapter 16 · What You Could Do This Year

Nobody is asking you to fix this. Here is some of what a person your age could do, in rough order of difficulty.

Start close

  • Find out whose land you are on. Do not stop at the name of the nation. Find out where those people are now, and what they are working on. Are they still nearby? Do they have a website, an event, a language class open to the public?

  • Read your own community’s records. If you are a Friend, ask to see your meeting’s minutes from the 1870s. If not, try your school’s, your town’s or your church’s. Look for what they found nothing to do about.

  • Ask an elder the question from Chapter 9. What did you learn about this, and when, and from whom?

  • Run the five questions on one part of your life. Use the worksheet in Appendix A. Choose something real: a job, a loan you are considering, a volunteer position.

Move a little

  • Show up somewhere, without an agenda. Go to a public event led by the people whose work it is. Sit in the back. Eat what is offered. Go again.

  • Take a shift at a mutual aid project. Many towns have one. When they offer you food, take some.

  • Move your money. If you have an account, consider a credit union.

  • Talk with a recruiter or lender with the five questions in hand. Read the terms. Know what you would be signing, whatever you decide.

  • Learn a skill people actually need. That means carpentry, canning, first aid, plumbing or bookkeeping. “No matter your skills, you are welcome,” the Hub’s call said, but it helps to have some.

If you are a Friend

  • Ask for a youth-led reading of the old minutes in your meeting, with permission from whoever holds them.

  • Ask who in your meeting already has a relationship with a nearby Indigenous or Black-led group. Ask them what they think, and do not go around them.

  • Ask for a clearness committee when you face a big decision about college, debt, enlistment or work. It is one of the most useful things Friends have, and it is underused for this.

  • Ask what your meeting is doing about its own land.

Queries to keep

Friends use queries in place of answers. These come from the author’s writing, some lightly adapted.

Where in my life am I giving without receiving, or receiving without giving?

Which of my daily needs reach me along a one-way channel? Who is on the other end?

Whose land am I standing on, and what have I returned?

Whose words and stories have carried my arguments? Have I asked them?

When I seek to convince, am I also willing to be convinced?

What are we doing to meet the survival needs of our wider community?

What has my own community already done well, and have we said so?

Where is a channel someone else began, that I could help carry, without taking it over?

Appendix C · Glossary

Accomplice. One who shares the risk of a struggle, by consent, accountable to the community most affected, and not merely supporting from a safe distance.

Active objection. Naming an injustice exactly, withdrawing from it where possible, and putting one’s energy into building what should replace it.

Advices and Queries. Friends’ way of offering counsel and questions in place of creeds.

Channel. A relationship strong enough to carry knowledge and care across a boundary: built over years, entered by invitation, carrying in both directions.

Charity. Giving that flows one way, in which the giver names the need, decides who qualifies, and writes the rules.

Conduit. A person through whom things are drawn from others along a one-way channel.

Conscientious objector. Someone who refuses military service on grounds of conscience.

Drained. A person from whom things are drawn along a one-way channel.

Economic draft. The arrangement by which a person’s survival is made to depend on entering one-way channels they did not open and cannot safely leave.

Extraction. A relationship in which things move in one direction only.

Friends. Members of the Religious Society of Friends, or Quakers.

LANDBACK. A movement led by Indigenous peoples for the return of land, stewardship and decision-making to them.

Meeting. A Quaker congregation. Monthly meetings belong to a larger yearly meeting. “Conservative” Friends are one branch, who keep unprogrammed worship and older practices.

Minute. A written record of a decision in a Quaker meeting.

Mutual aid. People coming together to meet each other’s needs, on the understanding that anyone may be helper or helped, outside the giver-receiver hierarchy.

NewCO. The author’s term for the New Conscientious Objector: a conscientious objector to extraction.

Node and terminal. From the United Panther Party’s framework: a node acts on its own decisions; a terminal receives on terms it did not write.

Peace Policy. U.S. Indian policy from 1869 under which Quakers and other church bodies were given charge of Indian agencies.

Protocol. The rules of a relationship, and who gets to write them.

Reciprocity. A relationship in which giving and receiving move in both directions and keep moving.

Two-row wampum. A Haudenosaunee agreement picturing two peoples traveling side by side, each in their own vessel, neither steering the other.
