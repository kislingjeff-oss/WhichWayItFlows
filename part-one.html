Part One · Why This Work, and Who It Is For

Chapter 1 · A Letter Before You Begin

From Three Concerns About Friends, the opening letter

Dear Friends, and dear readers who are not Friends,

This paper answers three concerns. They are simple to say and hard to live with.

The first is about poverty. In a land with more than enough, children go hungry, families have no home, and people despair. That is not an accident. It is how our economic system works, and we know it.

The second is about freedom. The land under our meetinghouses, and much of the comfort we enjoy, came from taking. Indigenous land was taken. The labor of enslaved people was taken. A legal and economic order was built to keep what was taken.

The third is about violence. The order that does the taking is held in place by force. That force has worn many uniforms: soldiers, Indian agents, boarding school disciplinarians, police, prison guards, border agents. Too often Friends have stood quietly near that force and called our quiet peace.

I wrote these concerns down first as a few short paragraphs, with a query under each one. I meant them for my own meeting, Bear Creek, a small rural Quaker meeting in Dallas County, Iowa. I also wrote a longer letter in 2023, An Epistle to Friends Regarding Community, Mutual Aid and LANDBACK. That letter ended with this sentence: “We must begin by changing our own lives if we hope to make a real testimony for peace and justice.”

This paper takes that sentence and those three concerns and tries to answer them, one at a time. It is written for young adult Friends, and for anyone else who wants to look honestly at the world we have been handed and ask what to do about it.

Why I am writing to young people

I am seventy-five years old. I grew up on rented farms near Bear Creek. My family was poor, but we didn’t know it, because everyone around us lived the same way. I went to Scattergood Friends School, a Quaker boarding school on a working farm near West Branch, Iowa, and graduated in 1970. I spent most of my adult life in Indianapolis, doing research on infant lung disease and development. I retired in 2017 and came back to Iowa.

Most of what I know about the three concerns in this paper I did not learn in school, in meeting for worship, or from books. I learned it from people. I learned it working in a church basement in Des Moines on Saturday mornings, packing boxes of food. I learned it walking ninety-four miles beside Native and non-Native friends along the route of an oil pipeline. I learned it swinging a sledgehammer inside an old house in Iowa City that will become a place of healing for Indigenous people.

Some knowledge can only be learned through relationships. I did not understand how devastating poverty is until I had spent years in friendship with people who live with it every day. I write to young people because you have more years ahead of you than I do to build those relationships. You also have something my generation often lacked: you have grown up already knowing that the systems around you are not working. Many of you have seen it in your own families. You do not need to be convinced that something is wrong. This paper asks what a person who believes in peace and justice, or a person of faith, does with that knowledge, in ways that are not just anger or despair.

Why I am also writing to others

This paper uses Quaker language because that is my language. I talk about “that of God in everyone,” about leadings, about testimonies and queries. But the three concerns are not only Quaker concerns. If you are not a Friend, you are welcome here. Where Quaker words appear, I try to explain them. Where the paper asks something of “Friends,” you can hear it as asking something of any community that says it cares about justice.

A question to carry through this paper

I have written a great deal over the years about capitalism. I still believe what I wrote. But arguments about economic systems have not changed many hearts. So I want to begin with a plainer question, one that does not require anyone to agree with me about economic theory first.

What does it say about a faith, or a religion, or a person, who has food and shelter and ignores the needs of their brothers and sisters?

And what does it say when they also ignore the voice telling them that their brothers’ and sisters’ need comes from what they themselves have taken?

That question runs through this whole paper. Each of the three concerns is a way of asking it again, more closely.

How this paper was made

This paper is built from my own writing over the past several years, from my 2023 Epistle, from records of my meeting and yearly meeting, from John Woolman’s A Plea for the Poor, and from a small number of public sources listed at the end. I had help assembling and drafting it from an AI model. I say that openly, because you should know how a thing you are reading was made. Any mistakes are mine, and I want to hear about them.

Some of the stories here belong to other people as much as to me. Where that is so, I have tried to tell only the part that is mine to tell.

Where I am now

In October 2026 I decided to step back from bringing my own proposals to my meeting’s business sessions. I had been doing that for years, and nothing much came of it. Instead I am putting my energy into person-to-person work. I have not left my membership there. I remain a member, and I conscientiously object to continuing “as we are” until the meeting takes up LANDBACK and ongoing work alongside Indigenous-led groups.

So this paper is not a proposal to any meeting for business. It is something closer to a long letter to friends I have not met yet. What you do with it is yours to discern.

In peace,

Jeff Kisling Sunday, November 1, 2026

Chapter 2 · The Stories We Are Proud Of

From Which Way It Flows, Chapter 1

If you grew up around Quakers, you probably know the stories. Friends hid people escaping slavery in false-bottomed wagons and under feather beds. Friends went to prison before they would fight. Friends fed the hungry children of an enemy nation after a world war, and in 1947 Friends received a Nobel Peace Prize for that kind of work.

If you did not grow up around Quakers, you may know the oatmeal box and not much else. That is fine. You can still read this, because the question underneath it is not only a Quaker one. Every family, school, town and country tells its best stories first. The question is what those stories are for.

This document makes an argument about that. Here it is, in four parts.

First, the good stories are real. They are worth knowing, and some of them are astonishing. Nothing here asks you to stop admiring the people in them.

Second, the good stories are incomplete. The same religious society that opposed slavery also ran schools built to erase Native children’s languages and families. Many Friends who took part in those schools believed they were serving peace. Quakers have talked a great deal about the first history and very little about the second.

Third, the good stories and the hard story turn out to be one story. When you look closely at what made the best moments work, and what made the worst moments do harm, you find the same question at the center of both: which way does it flow, and does it flow back?

Fourth, that question can be used to imagine something. People are already living by it, in small and imperfect ways, in food programs and work crews and on land that has come back to Indigenous hands. If that path keeps going, what does the world look like when you are old? This document offers one attempt at an answer.

Where this comes from

These pages are built from the writings of Jeff Kisling, a member of Bear Creek Meeting, Iowa Yearly Meeting (Conservative). Those writings include about ten years of blog posts, several long essays, a revised epistle to Friends, and a searching self-examination called Both Ledgers. From here on he is called “the author.” The pages are written about his writing, in the third person, so that you can tell where his words are being quoted and where they are being analyzed.

Nothing here has been considered or approved by Bear Creek Meeting, by Iowa Yearly Meeting (Conservative), or by any other body. It speaks for no one but its author. The people and organizations it describes did not review it and should not be held responsible for it.

Why a document like this is for you

Three reasons, none of them about guilt.

You are the one who will live in what comes next. The people who wrote most of the documents behind this one are old. The author is seventy-five. The arrangements they are questioning, including debt, land, water, and who gets to be safe, will fall on you for much longer than on them.

History has often been moved by people your age. Edwin Coppoc was raised among Quakers in Springdale, Iowa. He was twenty-four when he was hanged for joining John Brown’s raid on Harpers Ferry. Bayard Rustin, raised by a Quaker grandmother, spent two years in prison as a conscientious objector, then helped organize the March on Washington. The Quaker stories are full of the young, and full of elders who did not want to listen to them.

You can ask questions adults cannot easily ask. A seventeen-year-old who says, “Whose land was this before the meetinghouse?” is harder to brush off than a seventy-five-year-old with a proposal. The author learned this the slow way. He spent years bringing proposals to his meeting and finally concluded that persuasion had become a one-way channel of its own. You do not need a proposal. You only need the question.

A warning: this is not a story in which Quakers turn out to be the heroes after all. If it works, you will come away less interested in who the heroes were and more interested in what you are going to do.

A note on hard material

Part Four describes what happened at Indian boarding schools. The account here avoids graphic detail, but the subject is painful, and for some readers it is a family’s own history. If it is your family’s history, you already know more about this than these pages can tell you, and you owe no one your reaction to them. The National Native American Boarding School Healing Coalition (boardingschoolhealing.org) is led from within Native communities and offers resources. It is fine to skip that part or to read it with someone.

Chapter 3 · How This Work Came Together

From Refusing the One-Way Channel, A Note Before Beginning

This document gathers the work I have done on the New Conscientious Objector, the NewCO, over the last several years and reads all of it again through one lens: reciprocity and extraction. It is not a new framework laid on top of the old one. It is an attempt to show that the old one was always about this, and that saying so plainly makes it sharper, more honest, easier to act on, and easier for other Friends to test for themselves.

When I first described the NewCO, I said it was someone like me who objects to being trapped in systems of Christian colonial capitalist violence. I used that phrase for years, because it is the language oppressed communities in this land use to name what has been done to them, and one principle of justice work is that those communities define their oppression in their own terms. I still honor that. But I have come to see that, spoken by me to other Friends, the phrase gets in the way. Listeners stop at one of its words and never reach the rest. So this document sets the phrase aside, and says instead, in plain words anyone can test, what it was pointing at.

What it was pointing at turned out to be a simple question, which I found underneath every part of the NewCO once I began asking it: which way do things flow, and does anything flow back? When I asked that question of everything the NewCO had named, the economic draft, the three practices of LANDBACK, Mutual Aid and Abolition, the move from ally to accomplice, all of it fell into one shape. This document traces that shape. It also takes up, at length, a question the shape forces on Friends: why so much Quaker aid over the centuries took the form of charity, and why so little has changed despite the millions of dollars Friends have given.

What it draws on

  • My own account of the draft years, 1969 to 1972, and the Friends Volunteer Service Mission.

  • The NewCO papers of 2025 and 2026, especially The Conscription of Survival, Defining the Economic Draft, The Problem, As I Understand It, and the accomplice material prepared for the Decolonial Repair Network.

  • The recent reciprocity writing: How Knowledge Travels (June 27, 2026), Single Sentence (August 2026), Nodes and Channels (October 22, 2026), Economies of Extraction and Reciprocity (October 29, 2026), A Plea for the Poor, Continued and Both Ledgers (October 30, 2026), A Plea for Reciprocity (October 31, 2026), the Epistle to Friends Regarding Reciprocity as revised October 31, 2026, and Three Concerns About Friends (November 1, 2026).

  • John Woolman’s A Plea for the Poor, George Fox’s Journal, Joseph Rowntree’s 1904 memorandum, the Advices of Iowa Yearly Meeting (Conservative), and published histories of Quaker relief and Indian policy.

Cautions carried forward

Several of the NewCO papers were assembled with the help of AI research tools. Where those papers make factual claims, I treat them here as leads to check, not as finished history. Stories that belong first to other people, especially the ceremony at Grace’s grave in 2024, are told only in outline. The draft visitor guidelines I prepared for the Hub are not quoted, because whether they are accepted is the Hub’s decision.

I use the phrase active objection throughout, never the other word so often used for this kind of work. Chapter 29 explains why.

From Three Concerns About Friends, How to Use This Paper

Reading alone

If you are reading alone, read slowly. Each part follows the same shape: the extraction, how it is hidden, and the reciprocity that answers it. Each part ends with queries. A query is a question Quakers ask ourselves, not to get a quick answer, but to let it work on us. Try writing your own answer to one query each day. Come back to it a week later and see if it has changed.

Reading with others

This paper was written to be read in groups: a young adult Friends gathering, a Sunday school class for older teens, a campus group, a reading circle at a meeting, a group of friends who are not Quakers at all. The discussion plan near the end divides the paper into six sessions. Each session asks you to read one part ahead of time, share honestly, and try one small thing in the week that follows.

When you meet, try to listen more than you argue. Some of what is in this paper will make some readers uncomfortable. Discomfort is not a sign that something is wrong. It is often the first sign that something true has reached us.

A note on words

Friends and Quakers mean the same thing: members of the Religious Society of Friends.

Meeting can mean a gathering for worship, or the community that gathers. A monthly meeting is a local congregation. A yearly meeting is a larger body of monthly meetings that gathers once a year. My yearly meeting is Iowa Yearly Meeting (Conservative).

Testimony means the way Friends’ faith shows up in how we live: peace, simplicity, integrity, community, equality, and care for the earth.

Query means a question for reflection.

Mutual aid means people coming together, as equals, to meet each other’s needs. It is explained fully in Chapter 21.

LANDBACK means returning land, and the power to care for it, to Indigenous peoples. It is explained fully in Chapter 22.

Active objection is the term I use for refusing to take part in something wrong and working to replace it with something better. Friends have long used the term “conscientious objection” for refusing to fight in wars. I use active objection for refusing to go along with an economic and social order that harms people.

Extraction means taking, with nothing fair coming back. Reciprocity means giving and receiving, both ways, and keeping it going. Both are explained in Chapter 4.
